unit-1
------

theorem 8.1: Any comparisonsort algorithm requires omega(nlog(n)) comparisons in the worst case.
proof:
- The Permutation Fact: An array of n elements has n! (n-factorial) possible orderings. A sorting algorithm must be able to reach every single one of these as a potential output.
- The Tree Fact: If you map the algorithm's Yes/No comparisons onto a binary "decision tree," the maximum number of leaves (possible outcomes) for a tree of height h is 2^h.
- The Math (The Crunch): Since the tree must have at least enough leaves to cover all n! permutations, we get the inequality 2^h >= n!. If you take the logarithm of both sides (h >= log(n!)) and apply a mathematical rule called Stirling's approximation, it simplifies to show that the height $h$ (the number of comparisons) is bounded below by Omega(n log(n)).

Corollary 8.2: Heapsort and merge sort are asymptotically optimal comparison sorts.
proof:
- The Theoretical Floor: Theorem 8.1 established that the universe's mathematical speed limit for comparison sorting is Omega(nlog(n)). You cannot go faster.
- The Algorithm Ceiling: We already know from analyzing Heapsort and Merge Sort that their worst-case running time is O(nlog(n)).
- The Conclusion: Because their maximum running time (O) perfectly matches the lowest possible required time (Omega), they leave no room for improvement. Therefore, they are "asymptotically optimal."
